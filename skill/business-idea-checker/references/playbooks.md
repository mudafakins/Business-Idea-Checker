# Expert playbooks

The expertise behind every step. Read the shared principles always, and the section for the
step you're on.

## Where this comes from

Distilled from well-established practice in venture validation and early-stage
strategy. Principles are summarised in our own words, not quoted, and carry no
statistics we cannot source. Main influences:

  - Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test: learn from customers' past behaviour and
    commitments, not their opinions or promises.
  - David Bland and Alex Osterwalder, Testing Business Ideas; Osterwalder et al.,
    Value Proposition Design: test the riskiest assumption first, and rank
    evidence by strength (what people do beats what they say).
  - Eric Ries, The Lean Startup; Ash Maurya, Running Lean: small, time-boxed
    experiments with success and failure criteria set in advance.
  - Bill Aulet, Disciplined Entrepreneurship: a narrow beachhead market, a
    bottom-up market size, unit economics (lifetime value against the cost of
    winning a customer).
  - Clayton Christensen, Jobs to Be Done: customers "hire" a product to make
    progress; the real competitor is what they do today.
  - Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, Traction: test several channels cheaply,
    then focus on the one that works.
  - Michael Porter, Competitive Strategy; Hamilton Helmer, 7 Powers: lasting
    advantage needs something hard to copy.
  - Daniel Kahneman and Dan Lovallo; Bent Flyvbjerg (reference class forecasting);
    Gary Klein (pre-mortem): take the outside view and expect plans to run
    over cost and time.
  - Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella and Spina (2020, Management Science), a field
    experiment finding that founders who treat ideas as hypotheses to test make
    better decisions; Kerr, Nanda and Rhodes-Kropf (2014) on entrepreneurship
    as experimentation; Tom Eisenmann, Why Startups Fail.

## Shared principles (always)

- Evidence has a strength order. What people have done (paid, pre-ordered, re-bought, switched) beats what they say they will do; signed commitments beat sign-ups; sign-ups beat clicks; clicks beat survey answers; opinions and compliments count for almost nothing.
- Take the outside view first: how do similar businesses usually do, in this place and channel? Then adjust for this case. Founders' plans are usually optimistic: costs come in higher, sales slower and set-up longer than planned.
- Triangulate. Anything that could change the verdict needs two independent sources, or a top-down and a bottom-up figure that roughly agree.
- Beware survivorship bias: a famous brand's success does not show a newcomer can win.
- The real competitor is what the customer does today, including doing nothing or making do.

## Research (step 3)

- Market size: prefer bottom-up (reachable buyers x how often they buy x price) for the person's city and channel in the first year or two. Headline industry reports usually cover a much broader category; when you use one, say what it covers and that it is broad.
- Demand signals worth finding: marketplace review counts and ratings, best-seller ranks, how many sellers compete, search interest, active community questions, waiting lists, price stability. Review counts on leading listings are strong evidence that people buy.
- Competitors: cover the leaders and a few small or new sellers. Record each one's main claim, price per unit, pack size, rating and review count. Read their 1 and 2 star reviews for unmet needs; those are possible gaps.
- Unit costs: look for manufacturer and wholesale price lists, published marketplace fee schedules (commission, closing and shipping fees), payment fees, return and cash-on-delivery failure costs, and taxes on the sale.
- Cost to win a customer: look for advertising cost-per-click and conversion-rate benchmarks for the category and country, so the cost per new customer can be worked out (cost per click divided by conversion rate). Advertising cost of sale (ad spend as a share of all sales) is a different, much lower number; label it clearly if that is all you find. Report ranges as estimates.
- How long customers stay: repeat frequency, cancellation rates, and any natural end point (outgrowing it, a life stage ending, a one-time need).
- Rules: licences, product standards, labelling rules and advertising-claim rules that apply (for example in India: FSSAI for food, BIS standards, the Legal Metrology packaging rules, the ASCI advertising code, GST). Name the rule; elsewhere, find the local equivalent.

Research checklist for one idea, in the person's country and channel:
- 4 to 8 competitors (mostly direct, one or two substitutes), each with price, pack size or
  billing basis, main claim, and the page URL. Prefer the big retailers and marketplaces
  people there use.
- Market size and direction for the specific customer group, plus any natural end point
  for a customer (outgrowing it, a life stage ending, a one-time need).
- Demand signals; unit cost; selling costs per sale; cost to win a new customer; rules.
- Two sources for anything that could change the verdict. If local data is thin, use the
  closest comparable market and say which and why.

## Checking for clashes (step 4)

- A nice-to-have problem, or a customer described as "everyone".
- Me-too positioning: claims every competitor already makes (natural, safe, premium, fast, cheap) are the entry ticket, not a reason to switch.
- A difference that is easy to copy: same white-label supplier, a feature, a slightly lower price.
- Pricing well above the market with no proof customers will pay more, or below cost to "grow".
- Unit economics that forget channel fees, delivery, returns, discounts, payment fees or the cost of winning each customer.
- A one-off purchase that needs costly advertising for every sale.
- Customers who stop needing it after a short time, cutting how much each one is worth.
- Dependence on one marketplace, platform, supplier or algorithm.
- Claims that need proof or permission (natural, organic, clinically tested, baby-safe).
- Cash tied up in stock or minimum order quantities beyond the budget.
- A plan that needs more time, skills or money than the person has, or earns later than they need.

Raise a clash only where an answer conflicts with the findings or with the person's own
limits, and quote the numbers. A concern is amber; it is serious (red) only for a broken
non-negotiable, every sale losing money after costs, or a legal or safety issue that could
end the business. Missing or vague answers are not clashes.

## Answering the evidence questions (step 5)

- D1 is "yes" when competitors with real prices exist and people evidently buy from them.
- D2 is "yes" only with concrete buying evidence: review counts, sales ranks, sales volumes, waiting lists. A large industry figure alone is not evidence of active buying.
- M1: compare the reachable market in the person's place and channel with the sales the plan needs, not with a national headline figure.
- M2 follows growth or decline data for the customer group itself, not general optimism.
- C1, C2 and C3: a difference counts only if customers value it, competitors do not already claim it, and it is hard to copy (special access, a cost advantage, a licence, a community, network effects, a distribution channel others cannot use). Claims everyone makes do not count.
- F1 is "no" only when the idea as described clearly does something the person said they refuse to do, or misses a rule they set themselves.
- Money: cost_per_sale is every cost that comes with one more sale (making or buying it, packing, delivery, channel and payment fees, expected returns), but not advertising. A common rule of thumb is that a customer should be worth at least three times what it costs to win them.
- customer_cost is the cost to win one NEW paying customer. It is not advertising cost of sale (ad spend as a share of all sales, often called ACoS), which is averaged over repeat orders that needed no new ads and so looks far cheaper. When only cost per click is known, work it out as cost per click divided by the share of visitors who buy, and say so in note.
- For costs, take the cautious end of a benchmark range; for sales and repeat rates, the middle at most. Benchmarks from agencies or sellers of services lean optimistic.
- R1 is "no" only for a real blocker: the activity itself is prohibited, it is unsafe, or it needs a licence or rights the person realistically cannot get. Rules on how a business may advertise or find clients, claims that need proof, and licences or registrations that can be obtained are things to handle, not blockers: answer "yes" or "unclear" and say what must be done in note.
- When the evidence is missing, answer "unclear"; never assume the favourable answer.

## Writing next steps (step 6)

- The cheapest test goes after the riskiest assumption: the one that would end the idea and has the least evidence. Measure behaviour, not opinions: a page with the real price that takes pre-orders, a small batch sold on the real channel, doing the service by hand for the first customers, a pilot with 10 to 30 customers. Keep it short, small and inside the budget.
- Set the green gates and the kill switch before the test starts. Each is one measure, one number and a time window. Cover demand (for example conversion or repeat rate), money (cost to win a customer, what is kept per sale) and quality (rating, complaints, returns).
- First conversations with customers: ask what they did last time and what they spend today, never "would you buy this?"; don't pitch; count commitments (money, time, an introduction), not compliments.
- The nearest version that could work changes one thing: a narrower customer group, a different channel, a different price model (subscription, bundle, trial pack), a wider range that keeps customers longer, or a difference that is hard to copy.
- For what would change the verdict, imagine it is a year later and the idea failed: name the most likely cause, and the fact that would rule it out.
- Explain any business term in plain words the first time you use it.

## Follow-up questions (step 2)

- Ask about specifics and the past, not hopes: "What did the last customer pay, and for what?" beats "Would people pay?"
- Ask for one missing number or name at a time, in the person's terms.
